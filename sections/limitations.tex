\section{Model Limitations: Bias, Generalization, and Interpretability}

Benchmark results tell only part of the story. This section examines three persistent challenges: systematic bias, poor performance under distribution shift, and lack of interpretability.

\subsection{Algorithmic Bias and Fairness}

Systematic performance gaps across demographic groups are among the most troubling findings in diabetes AI \cite{wang2024ai, prioleau2025deep}. Prioleau et al. \cite{prioleau2025deep} found all six glucose prediction models made larger errors for women than men across three cohorts and multiple architectures—pointing to how systems are built and evaluated. Retinal screening shows similar disparities: training data mostly from high-income countries, variable performance in underrepresented populations \cite{wang2024ai, olusanya2024mitigating, zhang2025systematic}. Phene et al. \cite{phene2019artificial} in Zambia and Wolf et al. \cite{wolf2024access} in diverse pediatric populations show bias is not inevitable with deliberate validation.

Perhaps most troubling is the pattern Prioleau et al. \cite{prioleau2025deep} documented: models consistently underperform for patients with poor glucose control. These are precisely the patients who need the most help. If AI systems work best for people who are already doing well, they risk widening the gap between those who manage their diabetes effectively and those who struggle \cite{haider2024algorithmic, liu2025scoping}.

Bias stems from overrepresented training data, selection toward patients with better access, accuracy-focused optimization that sacrifices minority subgroups, and metrics that average across patients \cite{wang2024ai, fahmy2025exploring, oikonomou2023machine}. Addressing it requires diverse data with explicit targets \cite{fahmy2025exploring, bai2024federated}, fairness-aware algorithms (with accuracy trade-offs \cite{fahmy2025exploring}), and standard fairness audits by demographics and severity \cite{wang2024ai, liu2025scoping}.

\subsection{Generalization and Dataset Shift}

Models that perform well on their test set often fail in practice when deployment conditions differ from development \cite{prioleau2025deep, zhu2021deep, zhou2023population}. Prioleau et al. \cite{prioleau2025deep} found poor conceptual reproducibility when applying glucose models across datasets—same code and device type, but different titration, diet, and activity; models learned non-transferable, site-specific patterns. Device differences compound this: CGM sensors differ in noise, sampling, and calibration \cite{alhaddad2022sense, prioleau2025deep, xie2024processing}; research-grade and portable retinal cameras produce very different images \cite{zhang2025systematic, raj2024federated}. Most studies use same-source held-out data \cite{prioleau2025deep, jacobs2023artificial}; independent validation consistently shows degradation \cite{prioleau2025deep, wang2024ai}. Improving generalization requires domain adaptation \cite{contreras2018artificial, chen2024crossmodality}, multi-site or federated training \cite{bai2024federated, fahmy2025exploring, raj2024federated}, and mandatory external validation \cite{prioleau2025deep, wang2024ai}.

\subsection{Interpretability and Explainability}

Clinicians need to know why a model recommends an action \cite{jacobs2023artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}; for safety-critical decisions, unexplained predictions are hard to trust or debug \cite{contreras2018artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}. SHAP, attention weights, and saliency maps offer partial views \cite{lundberg2017shap, munoz2023shap, chadaga2024interpretable, alam2024machine, bhati2024interpretable, zhu2021deep} but are unstable, non-causal, or artifact-prone \cite{jacobs2023artificial, alam2024machine, zhu2021deep, mackenzie2023diabetes}. Inherently interpretable models (trees, rules, linear) trade accuracy \cite{contreras2018artificial, alam2024machine}; hybrid interpretable–opaque designs remain challenging \cite{zhu2021deep}. Regulators emphasize transparency and oversight \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial} but there is no consensus on adequate explanation or interpretability metrics \cite{jacobs2023artificial}.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Reporting Standards}

Prioleau et al. \cite{prioleau2025deep} reviewed 60 glucose-prediction papers and found code rarely available, methods incomplete, and results only on familiar benchmarks; heterogeneous preprocessing, metrics, and splits make comparison nearly impossible \cite{jacobs2023artificial}. Demographic reporting is often absent \cite{wang2024ai, prioleau2025deep}. TRIPOD and CONSORT-AI offer reporting standards \cite{wang2024ai, liu2025scoping}; code and data sharing and standard benchmarks with fixed protocols would improve reproducibility and comparability \cite{prioleau2025deep, jacobs2023artificial}.
