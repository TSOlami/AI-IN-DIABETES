\section{Introduction}

Diabetes is a defining health crisis. The International Diabetes Federation reports 537 million adults with diabetes in 2021, rising to 783 million by 2045 \cite{sun2022idf, idf2021atlas}. The disease consumes about 12\% of global health spending and causes serious complications including heart disease, kidney failure, and blindness \cite{idf2021atlas, guan2023artificial}. Current management relies on periodic clinic visits, finger-prick glucose tests, and one-size-fits-all plans that struggle to account for the many factors affecting each patient \cite{oikonomou2023machine, mackenzie2023diabetes}.

The rise of wearable technology and AI has opened new possibilities for diabetes care. Continuous glucose monitors now track blood sugar every few minutes, while smartwatches capture physical activity, heart rate, and sleep patterns \cite{alhaddad2022sense, rodriguez2023applications}. Retinal cameras can detect early signs of diabetic eye disease, which affects roughly one-third of people with diabetes and remains a leading cause of preventable blindness \cite{yau2012global, zhang2025systematic}. Electronic health records contain years of lab results, prescriptions, and clinical notes \cite{oikonomou2023machine}. Researchers have begun combining these data streams through AI systems that promise to predict glucose swings before they happen, catch complications earlier, and tailor treatments to individual patients \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial, lee2024multimodal}.

Moving these systems from research into real clinics has proven difficult: models that excel on benchmarks often fail on new hospitals, populations, or devices \cite{prioleau2025deep, zhu2021deep, alam2024machine}. Prioleau et al. \cite{prioleau2025deep} tested six glucose prediction models and found all made larger errors for women than men and worse for patients with poor glucose control. Wang et al. \cite{wang2024ai} documented similar disparities in retinal imaging for underrepresented groups. These biases reflect how data are collected, models trained, and success measured.

Low-resource settings sharpen these challenges. AI systems often assume reliable electricity, fast internet, and expensive devices—assumptions that do not hold in much of the world \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, ghosh2025artificial, olusanya2024mitigating}. LMICs carry a disproportionate diabetes burden but remain almost invisible in AI training data \cite{raj2024federated, wang2024ai}; models built for well-resourced clinics often produce unreliable results when faced with different disease patterns, lower-quality images, or intermittent data \cite{zhang2025systematic, olusanya2024mitigating}. Federated learning \cite{fahmy2025exploring, bai2024federated}, transfer learning, and domain adaptation \cite{contreras2018artificial, chen2024crossmodality} offer partial responses, and Bahmani et al. \cite{bahmani2025achieving} propose integrating AI with education and capacity building—but these approaches remain largely experimental where they are most needed.

This review examines obstacles from data fusion to deployment in resource-limited settings, analyzes what current methods can and cannot do, identifies gaps in bias, generalization, and interpretability, and highlights directions for more accurate, fair, and accessible AI-driven diabetes care.
