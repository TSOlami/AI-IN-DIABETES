\section{Open Research Gaps and Future Directions}

The challenges above point to specific research priorities in methods, data, validation, and policy.

\subsection{Methodological Advances}

\subsubsection{Robust and Generalizable Models}

Domain adaptation that accounts for source–target differences shows promise but is underexplored in diabetes \cite{fahmy2025exploring, chen2024crossmodality}; meta-learning could enable adaptation with minimal new data \cite{zhu2021deep}. Causal inference may improve generalization \cite{oikonomou2023machine, jacobs2023artificial} but applying it to high-dimensional time series and images remains challenging.

\subsubsection{Fairness-Aware Learning}

Fairness-aware training \cite{fahmy2025exploring, wang2024ai} involves trade-offs (e.g., improving one group may harm another \cite{fahmy2025exploring}); choosing acceptable trade-offs requires ethicists, clinicians, and communities \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, liu2025scoping}. Intersectional fairness is harder: a model can do well for women and for Black patients on average yet fail for Black women \cite{wang2024ai, haider2024algorithmic}, and methods and data for small subgroups are lacking.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Quantification and Calibration}

Uncertainty quantification (Bayesian deep learning, ensembles, conformal prediction) remains limited in diabetes AI \cite{contreras2018artificial, han2024gluensemble, chadaga2024interpretable}; calibration is often poor for rare events and unusual patients \cite{wang2024ai, liu2025scoping}. Models should express greater uncertainty on out-of-distribution data \cite{prioleau2025deep, jacobs2023artificial}; reliable quantification under shift is essential for safe deployment.

\subsubsection{Interpretable and Explainable AI}

Interpretable-by-design models (neural additive, concept bottleneck, prototype-based) and hybrid interpretable–opaque architectures are promising \cite{alam2024machine, bhati2024interpretable, zhu2021deep}. The field needs standardized interpretability metrics and human-centered studies of whether explanations help clinicians \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, jacobs2023artificial}.

\subsection{Data and Infrastructure}

\subsubsection{Diverse and Representative Datasets}

Diverse training data from underrepresented populations, multiple devices, and varied settings is essential \cite{wang2024ai, prioleau2025deep, bai2024federated} and requires addressing privacy, consent, and funding barriers \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}. Federated learning can enable global collaboration without centralizing data \cite{bai2024federated, fahmy2025exploring, raj2024federated}; challenges in efficiency, heterogeneity, and fairness across sites need further work \cite{fahmy2025exploring}.

\subsubsection{Standardized Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocols}

The field needs agreed benchmarks with fixed splits, standard metrics, and required reporting—including diverse populations and evaluation beyond aggregate accuracy (subgroups, calibration, robustness) \cite{prioleau2025deep, jacobs2023artificial, wang2024ai}. Mandatory reporting of demographics, preprocessing, and hyperparameters would improve reproducibility \cite{prioleau2025deep}.

\subsection{Clinical Translation and Implementation}

\subsubsection{Prospective Clinical Trials}

Most work is retrospective; prospective trials are needed to show AI improves outcomes \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, fahmy2025exploring}. Wolf et al.'s ACCESS trial \cite{wolf2024access} demonstrated one model. Trials should include diverse and low-resource settings \cite{ghosh2025artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}; pragmatic effectiveness designs are valuable for implementation \cite{guan2023artificial}.

\subsubsection{Human-AI Collaboration}

Human–AI collaboration—interface design, workflow, when to override, preserving skills and autonomy—remains poorly understood \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, jacobs2023artificial}.

\subsubsection{Health Economics and Cost-Effectiveness}

Resource-constrained health systems need evidence about whether AI tools are worth the investment. Rigorous health economic evaluations must account for device and infrastructure costs, ongoing operational expenses, and long-term savings from prevented complications \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, guan2023artificial, ghosh2025artificial}. Budget impact analyses are essential for low-resource contexts where affordability determines adoption.

\subsection{Regulatory and Policy Innovation}

\subsubsection{Adaptive Regulatory Frameworks}

Existing medical device regulations assume static technologies and struggle with AI systems that learn and change over time \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial}. New frameworks are needed that allow iterative updates while maintaining safety oversight. Risk-based approaches that tailor requirements to clinical impact could balance innovation with patient protection \cite{khalifa2024artificial}.

International harmonization could reduce duplicative approval processes and accelerate global access, though local adaptation may be necessary to account for different healthcare contexts \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, ghosh2025artificial}.

\subsubsection{Data Governance and Privacy}

Enabling research while protecting patients requires better governance frameworks. Federated learning, differential privacy, and secure computation offer technical solutions, but legal and institutional barriers persist \cite{bai2024federated, fahmy2025exploring, mackenzie2023diabetes}. Participatory models that involve patients and communities in decisions about data use may build trust and ensure alignment with stakeholder values \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsection{Education and Capacity Building}

AI education in curricula and capacity building in LMICs—where need is greatest and expertise most limited—are essential \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, bahmani2025achieving, ghosh2025artificial, fahmy2025exploring}. Bahmani et al. \cite{bahmani2025achieving} propose integrating education, research, and practice; international partnerships can support local development and validation \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsection{Emerging Technologies}

\subsubsection{Digital Twins and Personalized Simulation}

Digital twins create virtual patient representations for prediction and treatment optimization \cite{cappon2024digital}; integrating CGM, wearables, and clinical history could enable simulated treatment response and dosing optimization. Validation and generalization to diverse populations need further work.

\subsubsection{Large Language Models}

Large language models could support education, self-management, and documentation, especially where healthcare workers are scarce \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, li2024integrated, ghosh2025artificial}; accuracy, bias, and safety require careful validation before deployment \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsubsection{Advanced Sensors}

Non-invasive and multi-analyte sensors could expand continuous monitoring and enable AI-driven closed-loop systems \cite{alhaddad2022sense, rodriguez2023applications}; accuracy, calibration, biocompatibility, and cost remain barriers \cite{alhaddad2022sense, mackenzie2023diabetes}.
