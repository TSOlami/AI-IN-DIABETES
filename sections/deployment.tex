\section{Deployment and Accessibility Challenges in Low-Resource Settings}

Deploying AI in clinical practice is difficult everywhere; obstacles multiply in resource-limited settings. LMICs bear a disproportionate diabetes burden yet receive little attention in AI development \cite{sun2022idf, olusanya2024mitigating}.

\subsection{Infrastructure and Connectivity Constraints}

Most AI systems assume stable internet, reliable electricity, and computing power \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, ghosh2025artificial, desai2025barriers}; Eze et al. \cite{eze2022sustainability} found infrastructure failures among the most common reasons digital health interventions fail in LMICs. Paper records remain standard in many facilities \cite{raj2024federated, ghosh2025artificial}; where EHRs exist they often lack standardized coding, identifiers, and interoperability \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, olusanya2024mitigating}. Mobile apps can deliver decision support without extensive IT \cite{rodriguez2023applications, ghosh2025artificial, alam2024machine} but are not universal and require digital literacy \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsection{Device Costs and Economic Barriers}

The devices that generate data for AI systems remain far too expensive for most patients in LMICs. CGM systems cost \$200-400 per month in high-income countries, an impossible expense where annual health spending per person is often below \$100 \cite{ghosh2025artificial, idf2021atlas}. Even basic glucometers and test strips create ongoing costs that many families cannot sustain \cite{alhaddad2022sense}.

Retinal imaging faces similar economics. Desktop fundus cameras typically cost \$10,000-16,000, while portable handheld alternatives range from \$3,000-9,000 \cite{raj2024federated, zhang2025systematic}. The cheaper devices produce lower-quality images, which degrades AI performance, creating a tradeoff between accessibility and accuracy \cite{zhang2025systematic}. The shortage of trained camera operators and ophthalmologists compounds the problem \cite{zhang2025systematic, guan2023artificial}.

AI may reduce long-term costs by preventing complications, but upfront investment is often prohibitive \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, guan2023artificial}; creative financing and technology transfer will be needed \cite{ghosh2025artificial}.

\subsection{Data Scarcity and Population Representativeness}

AI models learn from data, and the data overwhelmingly comes from wealthy countries. Raj et al. \cite{raj2024federated} and Olusanya et al. \cite{olusanya2024mitigating} document the severe underrepresentation of LMIC populations in publicly available training sets. Models optimized for European or North American patients may not work well for people with different genetics, diets, and disease patterns \cite{wang2024ai, zhang2025systematic}.

DR prevalence and progression differ across populations \cite{yau2012global, zhang2025systematic}; diet and lifestyle vary, so models trained on Western data may fail elsewhere \cite{ghosh2025artificial}. Local validation is essential but resources are scarce. Transfer learning \cite{contreras2018artificial, chen2024crossmodality} and federated learning \cite{bai2024federated, fahmy2025exploring} offer paths forward; Phene et al. \cite{phene2019artificial} showed a Singapore-trained system performed well in Zambia. Negative transfer—assumptions from one setting hurting another—remains a risk \cite{prioleau2025deep}.

\subsection{Clinical Validation and Regulatory Frameworks}

Trials in low-resource settings face infrastructure, funding, and expertise hurdles \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, fahmy2025exploring}; pragmatic implementation studies help but rarely establish causality \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}. Many LMICs lack AI medical device guidelines \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial}; well-resource-oriented regulations can be impractical elsewhere \cite{ghosh2025artificial}. Evidence from high-income settings does not automatically apply \cite{cappon2024digital, fahmy2025exploring}; local validation is essential and building that capacity is a major undertaking \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsection{Workforce Capacity and Training}

Specialists who could oversee AI-assisted care—endocrinologists, ophthalmologists, data scientists—are in short supply in many LMICs \cite{guan2023artificial, ghosh2025artificial, raj2024federated}. Primary care delivers most diabetes care but may lack training or confidence to integrate AI \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}; hesitancy over accuracy, liability, and workflow is legitimate \cite{guan2023artificial}. Trust requires hands-on training, transparent communication, and evidence of benefit \cite{jacobs2023artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}. Bahmani et al. \cite{bahmani2025achieving} propose integrating research, education, and practice to democratize AI knowledge; technology alone cannot achieve equity without trained people and strong institutions \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, bahmani2025achieving}.

\subsection{Ethical and Socio-Cultural Considerations}

Systems built in high-income settings may not address local priorities or values; deployment without local validation risks imposing solutions rather than developing in partnership \cite{ghosh2025artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}. Privacy concerns are heightened where laws and security capacity are weak \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial}; consent and scrutiny of commercial interests matter \cite{fahmy2025exploring}. Community engagement can align AI with local needs \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes}. Cultural factors—local foods, religious practices, language, literacy—must shape recommendations and interfaces \cite{ghosh2025artificial, mackenzie2023diabetes}.

\subsection{Pathways to Equitable AI Deployment}

Making AI work in low-resource settings requires deliberate effort on multiple fronts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Technology adaptation:} Design devices and algorithms for resource-constrained environments, including edge computing that works without constant internet and models that remain useful with lower-quality input data \cite{fahmy2025exploring, ghosh2025artificial, zhu2024population}.
    \item \textbf{Capacity building:} Invest in training healthcare workers, researchers, and data scientists who can adapt, validate, and maintain AI systems locally \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, bahmani2025achieving}.
    \item \textbf{Collaborative research:} Use federated learning and multi-site partnerships to include diverse populations in model development while respecting data sovereignty and privacy \cite{bai2024federated, fahmy2025exploring, raj2024federated}.
    \item \textbf{Inclusive design:} Involve patients, clinicians, and communities in developing systems to ensure cultural appropriateness and practical usability \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, bahmani2025achieving}.
    \item \textbf{Policy innovation:} Create regulatory frameworks and financing mechanisms suited to local contexts, balancing safety requirements with the need for accessible technology \cite{mackenzie2023diabetes, khalifa2024artificial}.
\end{itemize}

Without attention to these priorities, the benefits of AI in diabetes care will accrue primarily to those who already have good access to healthcare, widening rather than narrowing global health disparities \cite{fahmy2025exploring, wang2024ai, haider2024algorithmic}.
